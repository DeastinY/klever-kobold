Teaching a model Pathfinder 2e

A survey of what exists, what the data allows, and why the benchmark has to be built before the model.

Retrieval-first Qwen3.8-27B QLoRA RTX 5090 · 32GB ORC / CUP

The finding

prior art

Nobody has fine-tuned a language model on Pathfinder 2e. Zero models on HuggingFace, zero rules or lore datasets, and no PF2e benchmark of any kind. The only PF2e artifacts on the Hub are one person's embedding and reranker pair — retrieval components, not a model. On GitHub, a handful of 0–2 star hobby RAG bots, none evaluated.

The community's own diagnosis is the right problem statement: general models fail at PF2e not from missing data but from D&D 5e contamination. 5e dominates the training distribution, so models answer Pathfinder questions confidently in the wrong system.

RAG or fine-tune is the wrong question

architecture

Two failure modes sit behind one complaint, and they have different fixes.

Failure one

Doesn't know Vicious Swing's damage scaling at level 10.

Retrieval.

41,743 discrete entities with exact numbers is a lookup problem. No fine-tune reliably memorises 41k stat blocks, and errata makes weights stale the week it ships.

Failure two

Answers with bonus actions, death saves, and advantage.

Fine-tuning.

That is a corrupted prior, not a missing fact. Retrieval does not remove a prior; it competes with one, and the prior often wins.

Measured: wrong. Retrieval removed the prior. The 9B's grounding went 9% → 81% with five excerpts in the prompt. The adapter's real job turned out to be excerpt selection and abstention.

Retrieval fixes facts. Fine-tuning fixes priors. So: retrieval carries the load, and a small LoRA does the things retrieval cannot — enforce Remaster vocabulary, and teach grounded citation and abstention.

Both halves now measured, and the second one did not survive. Retrieval's case is overwhelming — it roughly triples every model. But it also fixed the priors it was not supposed to fix: given the right rules entry, even the 9B stops reaching for 5e. What retrieval does not fix, and actively makes worse, is knowing which excerpt to use and when to admit the answer is in none of them. See Results.

The literature agrees, and points at the hybrid rather than either pole. RAFT trains on retrieved context including distractors, with answers that quote the source verbatim — a near-perfect fit, since "cite the entry you used" is the target behaviour. EntiGraph synthetic pretraining recovers over 80% of RAG's gain parametrically and stacks with it; Synthetic Mixed Training measures +2.6% over RAG alone but +9.1% combined. And on small corpora specifically, plain continued pretraining barely moves the needle — diverse synthetic rephrasing is what lands.

Corpus

64,347 chunks · 19.1M tokens

Archives of Nethys serves its search index from an anonymously readable Elasticsearch cluster, one document per game entity, each carrying a pre-rendered markdown field. It is the cleanest Pathfinder rules text available anywhere. PathfinderWiki adds Golarion lore through the MediaWiki API — pulled as raw wikitext rather than rendered extracts, because the infoboxes carry structured lore the extract endpoint throws away.

The must_not_contain lists are the design decision worth defending. Contamination is detectable by substring — "bonus action", "death saving throw", "advantage" — so the headline number needs no LLM judge, which keeps it cheap, reproducible, and not itself model-dependent.

Four bugs in my own metric

Every one was found by reading model responses rather than model scores, and every one is now a case in eval/test_score.py. The pattern is worth stating plainly: a benchmark's scorer is code, it has bugs, and the bugs are invisible from the leaderboard.

  1. Question echo. "Does the game have an Insight skill?" cannot be answered without the phrase. Scored four correct refutations as contamination.
  2. Denial counted as use. "Pathfinder does not use hit dice" is the right answer.
  3. Empty responses scored clean. Under a negative check, a truncated run looked like a perfect one. gpt-5 had 15 truncations, five of them in that family.
  4. The denial window punished articulate models. A four-word window read "characters no longer have rules-defined labels like lawful good" as a leak. Widening it to eight then let a real leak through — "did not critically succeed, they take half damage" — so the rule now also refuses to cross a clause terminator, or a comma followed by a fresh subject.

The first three flattered the project's premise. The fourth penalised exactly the models that explain themselves well, which is why the current frontier model looked worse than gpt-5 on contamination until it was fixed.

And one bug in the benchmark itself

Diagnosing why retrieval capped at 65% on remaster_rename while every other family sat at 97–100% found the cause in the questions, not the retriever. AoN's legacy_name is many-to-one: "Tanglefoot Bag" is claimed by Glue Bomb and all four of its grades, so demanding one particular grade marks a correct answer wrong. Every claimant is now acceptable. And 27 items had a false premise outright — the "old" name is still a live entry, so "what was Invisibility renamed to?" has no answer. Those are excluded rather than scored, which is why the benchmark reports 459 of 486.

Chunks are entity-granular, never token windows — every AoN document is already one atomic game object. Lexical and dense rankings are fused by reciprocal rank, which sidesteps making a BM25 score of 31.4 commensurable with a cosine of 0.82.

The hop

One family resisted everything: remaster_rename sat at 46% while every other family was at 97–100%. The reason is structural. Those questions name the old entity, so both retrievers land squarely on the legacy chunk — the one that must never be served as current rules.

Filtering legacy content out up front makes it worse, because that chunk is the only one that knows where the answer went: it stores a remaster_id pointing at its replacement. So the fix is to hop rather than to filter — retrieve the legacy entry, then hand back the entry that superseded it. Combined with fixing the benchmark's own many-to-one ambiguity, that took the family from 46.2% to 88.7% and overall R@5 to 97.1%. It only exists because the corpus build kept the cross-references.

The embedder bake-off has an awkward answer

Kaylebor/pf2e-codex-embed-xs is the only PF2e-adapted retriever anyone has published, so the honest control is its own base model, Snowflake/snowflake-arctic-embed-xs. They score identically — 72.4 / 86.1 / 91.7 R@1/5/20, MRR 0.784, to three decimals across every query.

That is not a coincidence. Comparing the two checkpoints tensor by tensor, the largest weight difference anywhere is 3.1×10−4, and the two sets of corpus embeddings have a mean cosine similarity of 1.0 with a maximum element difference of 7×10−5. Whatever separates the fine-tune from its base is at the magnitude of a precision round-trip. On this benchmark it conveys no measurable advantage.

The practical upshot is cheerful anyway: a 22M-parameter embedder that indexes the whole corpus in 28 seconds is within half a point of Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, which takes 213 seconds. The retrieval work here is mostly lexical, and the corpus is small.

Phase 01 — closed book

Everything lands between 15.8% and 25.3%. Lookup is hopeless at any scale, and on rarity every model scores below the trivial baseline — answering "uncommon" to all eighty gets 70%, gpt-5 got 31%. Contamination showed up exactly where it was supposed to: Qwen3.8-27B put death saving throws and an invented "Short Rest: typically 1 hour" into Pathfinder rulings, and Qwen3.5-9B named the correct Pathfinder machinery in 9% of rulings against gpt-5's 78%. A thinking-mode control ruled out the obvious confound.

Phase 02 — with the book open

Retrieval roughly triples every model, and the local-versus-frontier gap nearly closes:

gpt-567.9% Qwen3.5-9B65.0% gpt-4.1-mini63.2% Qwen3.8-27B63.2%

With five retrieved excerpts. Closed-book, the same four scored 25.3 / 15.8 / 21.4 / 21.8.

A 9B model running in 4-bit on one desktop card is 2.9 points off gpt-5. That is the single most useful number produced so far, and it was produced by indexing, not training.

The thesis was half wrong, and this is the half. "Retrieval fixes facts, fine-tuning fixes priors" — retrieval fixed the priors too. Qwen3.5-9B's applied-trap grounding went from 9% to 81% and its quiz leaks from nine to zero. Every model is now at or above 87.5% clean. A model reading the correct rules entry does not reach for D&D 5e; it did not need its prior corrected, it needed the page.

Retrieval also created a new failure

Abstention collapsed. gpt-5 went from 77.5% to 17.5%, and Qwen3.8-27B from 77.5% to 0.0% — it now invents a level for 27 of 40 feats that do not exist, having invented three before. Handed five plausible neighbouring feats and asked about one that was never written, a model answers about a neighbour. Retrieval does not just fail to fix this; it makes it substantially worse, because the distractors are now sitting in the prompt looking authoritative.

Where the remaining errors actually are

With the grader fixed, retrieval-augmented accuracy is 81.9–84.3% and the failures concentrate almost entirely in one family. Qwen3.5-9B with five excerpts, by family:

5e traps96.7% level96.2% rarity96.2% applied traps93.8% traits87.5% prereq82.5% remaster73.6% abstention35.0%

Abstention is the whole remaining problem. Everything else sits between 73% and 97%. And it is not a local quirk: RefusalBench (EACL 2026) evaluated 30+ models and found refusal accuracy below 50% on multi-document tasks, with neither scale nor extended reasoning helping. A controlled 2026 study found small RAG models abstain 97–99% of the time when context is simply missing, and collapse to answering 13.6–74.3% of the time when it is misleading. Five plausible neighbouring feats is the misleading case exactly — which is why retrieval made this family worse rather than better.

The current frontier, closed-book

gpt-6-astra is the strongest closed-book result at 31.6%, and the only model that never fabricated a level — 0 of 40, against gpt-5's 1, Qwen3.8-27B's 3, Qwen3.5-9B's 21 and gpt-4.1-mini's 33. Its applied-trap grounding is 88%, the best measured. It still scores 16.2% on feat levels: the frontier has moved on calibration, not on knowing this corpus. Its retrieval run is the one missing cell in the table — the API account ran out of credits partway through.

What phase 03 is now for. Not the 5e prior, which retrieval removed, and not precision, which was mostly a grading bug. One job: refuse when the answer is in none of the excerpts, without becoming so cautious it stops answering the other 92% correctly. RefusalBench's finding that selective refusal is "a trainable, alignment-sensitive capability" is the reason to expect this to work; the Divide-Then-Align finding that RAFT itself trains models to answer when knowledge is absent is the reason to watch it closely.

Model and hardware

32GB, sm_120

Baseline is Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B — Apache-2.0, 27.78B dense parameters, 64 layers, 262k context, released 5 August 2026. At 4-bit that is roughly 14.5 GB of weights, leaving room for QLoRA at moderate sequence length on a single RTX 5090. The 248k vocabulary makes the loss head unusually memory-hungry, so chunked cross-entropy matters more than it normally would.

The other Qwen3.8 checkpoints are not local options: Flash-Next is 180B MoE and 2.4T-A95B is the flagship. The FP8 variant is for serving, not training. If 27B QLoRA proves too tight to iterate on daily, Qwen3.5-9B has a base checkpoint and trains fast.

Measured, not estimated: loaded in nf4 4-bit it occupies 22.9 GB of the 32 GB card and answers all 486 benchmark items in 11m45s greedily at 500 tokens, batch 16. There is comfortable headroom for QLoRA at moderate sequence length. Note the checkpoint is multimodal (Qwen3_5ForConditionalGeneration) and Qwen has shipped no -Base variant — fine for RAFT SFT off the instruct weights, an argument for the 9B if phase 04 continued pretraining happens.

Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B answers the iteration-cost question: 486 items in 4m41s against the 27B's 11m45s, two and a half times faster, and it has twice as much contamination to fix — which arguably makes it the better testbed for proving the adapter does anything.

Ceiling check, done. gpt-5 closed-book scores 25.3% against the 27B's 21.8%. The frontier advantage is real but small, and it is concentrated in abstention and calibration rather than knowledge — nobody knows this corpus. That is the case for retrieval, and it is why the fine-tune only has to buy behaviour.

Plan

gated phases

Each phase produces a number that decides whether the next one is worth doing. The ordering is the point: the benchmark before the model, retrieval before the tune.

00

Corpus done

Both dumps, normalisation to 64,347 chunks, the 470-item benchmark. Nothing derived is committed; everything rebuilds from four scripts.

01

Baseline measurement done

Scorer built, all graders deterministic. Three closed-book runs plus a thinking-mode control. Scoring surfaced three false-positive bugs in the contamination metric — a forbidden term echoed from the question, a term inside an explicit denial, and empty responses scoring clean under a negative check — all of which flattered the premise. Fixed and audited.

Exit criterion — answeredFrontier models are clean at 30/30; Qwen3.8-27B is not, at 25/30. The fine-tune has a target, and it is a specific one.
02

Retrieval done

Entity-granular chunks, hybrid BM25 and dense fused by reciprocal rank, and a legacy-to-Remaster hop that reads the cross-references the corpus build preserved. R@5 of 92.5%. The embedder bake-off found the only published PF2e retriever to be numerically indistinguishable from its own base model.

Exit criterion — answeredRetrieval recall is 88–96% on the families where accuracy is 21–30%. That gap is not a prior problem, it is a precision problem: the right chunk is in the context and the model answers from the wrong one.
03

RAFT LoRA next

Train on retrieved context, not raw rules text: question, gold chunk plus distractors, and an answer that quotes the source and cites its URL. Deliberately include unanswerable questions, legacy-name questions whose correct answer is the Remaster name, and items where every retrieved chunk is a distractor.

Exit criterion — revised after phase 02Contamination is off the list; retrieval solved it. The adapter has to move prereq and lookup_traits up toward their retrieval ceilings, and drag abstention back from the collapse retrieval caused, without regressing anything else.
04

Lore pretraining gated

Only if phase 03 shows the model can hold Pathfinder framing. Lore is where parametric knowledge is actually appropriate — narrative, forgiving, few exact numbers — and where the 403k-edge entity graph earns its keep. Needs a lore eval that does not exist yet.

05

Serving queued

vLLM on the FP8 weights, or GGUF for a smaller footprint. Surface it where it gets used: an MCP server, or a Foundry VTT module. Every answer cites its source URL — that is both the trust mechanism and the attribution mechanism.

Licensing

not legal advice

Rules mechanics are ORC-licensed — reusable with attribution, under a license stewarded by Azora Law rather than owned by Paizo. Golarion lore and PathfinderWiki sit under Paizo's Community Use Policy: non-commercial, freely available use only.

  • Personal local use of a fine-tuned model is fine. Nothing in Paizo's license pages addresses AI training either way, and non-commercial personal research is squarely what the CUP contemplates.
  • Publishing lore-trained weights is genuinely grey. A weight file cannot carry the CUP's attribution requirements in any meaningful sense.
  • The benchmark is the safe artifact — questions and structured answers with a source URL on every item.

Paizo banned AI-generated content in its own products and marketplaces in 2023. That governs Paizo's publications, not a personal fine-tune, but it does predict how the community would receive published weights.
